Executive summary

Short answer. Any ordinary app, with no device management, can really block chosen apps on iOS and Android: during a focus session, on a schedule, or after a daily limit. It can then unlock them when the user completes a task the app verifies. What no app can do is stop an adult from switching protection off on their own phone. The right product is a commitment device with real friction, honestly described.

What decides the design

  • iOS uses Apple's Screen Time API; iOS itself enforces the block [1][9][18].
    • Needs a paid developer team, even to test on a phone [8].
    • Apple must approve the app and each of its 3 extensions [6].
    • Limits: 50 app tokens per store (one report says across all stores), 20 schedules, 15-minute minimum windows [10][16][27].
  • Android has no blocking API: usage access + display over other apps + a foreground service. Exact and flexible, but a force stop ends it [34][37][42]. Avoid accessibility services (Play policy, and Android 17's Advanced Protection) [54][62].
  • Biggest risk: Apple's March 2026 rule requires the app's primary purpose to be focus or personal device-usage management [23]. An app with another main purpose must present the feature that way, and approval isn't guaranteed.
  • Known bug: iOS usage-threshold events misfire (reported from iOS 17.6.1 through 26.x and 27), and Apple has promised no fix [90][25][91]. Schedules and focus sessions aren't affected.

Before launch the work is mostly not code: a paid Apple team, Apple's approval for the app and each extension (reported waits from a few days to three weeks or more), Play declarations, and a pass on real phones.
